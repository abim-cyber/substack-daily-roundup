The AI PM Playbook: Building AI Products at Scale

The AI PM job has shifted away from shipping features and toward owning the eval. Here’s everything from our lightning lesson with Shyamala Prayaga, Senior AI Product Manager at NVIDIA.

Shyamala Prayaga has spent over two decades in product, most of it in conversational and voice AI: Google, Microsoft, Adobe, and now NVIDIA, where she leads conversational and generative AI products. She’s shipped voice interfaces for cars, wearables, and connected homes, wrote a book on trustworthy digital assistants, and now owns roadmaps for AI systems that answer differently every time you ask them.

What separates someone who talks about AI products from someone who ships them comes down to one skill: defining what “good” means when the product is non-deterministic.

What the AI PM role actually is now

Traditional PM assumes stable behavior. Write a spec, engineering builds to it, QA checks the build against it. That loop breaks the moment your product is a model.

A model doesn’t conform to a spec. It samples from a distribution of possible outputs. So the PM’s center of gravity moves toward defining and defending how quality gets measured, rather than defining the feature itself. If you take one thing from this session, take that.

Why the playbook is still being written

  • The role is new and the talent gap is huge. AI PM is one of the fastest-growing product roles, and there’s no textbook. Everyone is figuring it out live, on production systems, right now.

  • The old skills don’t fully transfer. Roadmaps, funnels, and support tickets all assume the product behaves the same way twice. AI products don’t. A prompt change can fix one request and quietly break another while every dashboard stays green.

  • The center of the job moved. The differentiated skill in 2026 goes beyond strategy or user empathy. It’s the ability to look at a pile of production outputs and say, precisely, what’s broken and how you’d measure the fix.

The MAP framework: Model, Augment, Program

  • Shyamala’s organizing model for building AI products is MAP: Model, Augment, Program. Do you use the raw model, augment it with retrieval and tools and context, or program hard constraints around it.

  • It reads like an architecture menu, but each rung is really a trade between capability and control:

    • Model: the most range, the least predictability. Good for open-ended surfaces like brainstorming.

    • Augment: RAG, tool calls, and structured context reclaim ground on faithfulness, at the cost of complexity and latency.

    • Program: hard guardrails buy you determinism exactly where you can’t afford surprises, and nowhere else.

  • The mistake is treating this as a one-time architecture pick. It’s a per-surface decision. Your billing flow wants Program. Your ideation assistant wants Model. Same product, opposite ends of the ladder. Treat MAP as a dial for how much non-determinism each part of your product is allowed to have.

Evaluating AI in production is the whole job

The session gave evaluation its own set of questions for a reason: how to evaluate AI in production, how to benchmark models, how to separate real impact from noise, and how to avoid AI hype. Four separate blocks, all circling the same anxiety. How do you know it’s actually working.

  • Don’t judge by eyeballing outputs. Reading a few responses and filing feedback was the old PM reflex. It doesn’t scale and it doesn’t catch regressions.

  • Build graded test sets and write scorers. Code-based scorers for the deterministic stuff (schema, format, length). LLM-as-judge scorers for what code can’t see (tone, faithfulness).

  • Keep the judges narrow. One failure mode per judge, validated against your own human labels before you trust it. A judge that asks “is this good?” tells you nothing. A judge that asks “does this cite a source that doesn’t exist?” gives you something to act on.

In 2026, the PM owns the eval outright, all the way from defining it to defending its results.

Rethinking UX: trust is the metric nobody measures

A model can score well on your evals and still harm the user. The failure lives in how much the user believes the output, well beyond whether the output is correct.

  • Research this year shows that simply labeling advice as “AI-generated” makes people accept recommendations they’d otherwise reject, and that heavy AI reliance erodes people’s confidence in their own judgment.

  • An interface can pass

    every usability test and still be training its users to stop thinking.

  • For an AI PM, that means an eval suite measuring only whether the model is right is incomplete. It also has to measure whether the user is calibrated: trusting the good answers, catching the bad ones. That calibration is a product surface. It’s the confidence signals you show, the friction you add before a high-stakes action, the moments you

    make the AI say “I’m not sure.” Trust miscalibration is the failure mode standard product metrics were built to never notice.

Breaking in: the wrong question and the right one

The session drew a lot of “how do I transition into this role” questions, from project managers, from data scientists, from adjacent roles. The honest through-line:

  • Learning to talk about models won’t get you in. Everyone can say “agents” and “RAG” and “context window.”

  • You transition by being the person who has actually written a rubric, argued with an LLM judge’s scores, and shipped a change they could prove helped.

The bottom line

The line between technical and non-technical PM is vanishing. In 2026, the AI PMs who stand out can tell you, with a number, why last week’s model swap made the product worse, even though every dashboard said it was fine.

The real deliverable is the eval.
